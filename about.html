About

Sheersh Kachhwaha

Why

Sheersh's starting point is renewable energy, not business. The pull came from watching where the world is actually heading: oil and gas carry a level of political controversy, pollution, and historical conflict over resources that he doesn't believe the next chapter of the energy story needs to repeat, while renewable energy is a far more believable growth story as the innovation gap keeps closing. An MSc in Renewable Energy Engineering from Kingston University and a BSc in Environmental Science from Fergusson College, Pune, both sit on that conviction rather than a conventional business track; and it still shapes how he reads a client problem. Sustainability isn't a department to consult once the real analysis is done; it's the lens the numbers get run through first. "ESG is dead" reads, to him, as a description of a label and some mandates, not of the exposure underneath; weather losses, supply-chain emissions and claim liability don't retreat just because the rules or the terminology do.

How

That conviction only matters if it survives contact with a spreadsheet. At Genpact, he supports structured, hypothesis-led analysis; MECE frameworks, issue trees; on cross-industry client advisory engagements, the same rigour he developed academically now tested in a live, client-facing setting. Independently, it shows up as a comparative investment case across renewable and conventional energy systems (CapEx, OpEx, LCOE, discount rate, carbon footprint), a lifecycle model spanning wind and solar assets, and a procurement analysis that turned system-efficiency modelling into a concrete material-substitution recommendation. He's increasingly applying AI-augmented and agentic workflows to that process; sped up without losing rigour; and treats technical detail and executive-ready narrative as two versions of the same output, not separate skills. The split he works to is specific: agents take the mechanical half; unit conversion, factor mapping, first drafts; while materiality, assurance and sign-off stay human, and every agent output is treated as a draft until a named person signs it. A number is expected to carry its source and the action it triggered, not just its total.

Strategy

The strategy is to keep sitting at the intersection of three things converging whether or not companies are ready for them: sustainability reporting, retail/CPG decision-making, and agentic AI. At Genpact, that means organising delivery across Finance, Real Estate, and Blueprinting & Solution Identification workstreams, and presenting agentic, Goal-as-a-Service solutions to client stakeholders; work that has to be planned and sold as carefully as it's built. Independently, it means writing in public about where that intersection is actually heading (sheersh13.substack.com, now mostly ESG and sustainability reporting), and keeping the project portfolio quantified rather than aspirational; so a case for reallocating capital toward renewables, or automating ESG's mechanical work while keeping judgment human, rests on real numbers, not a trend he's chasing. Value, in his view, compounds through shared infrastructure rather than one-off automation; a single data and agent layer that makes the next use case cheaper, not four separate projects that each pay once; and the same scrutiny applies to the tools doing the work, since AI earns trust through auditability and narrow, revocable authority, not by assertion. He treats refining this thinking as a matter of course, not a one-time positioning exercise.

Outside of work, badminton sits alongside a deeper, ongoing interest in nutrition, diet, fitness and sport, one that traces back to watching his mother work as a nutritionist; it turns out to be directly useful in CPG and retail work, where understanding the product; and how a consumer actually experiences it once it's on the shelf; matters as much as understanding the client. He's also fluent in English and Hindi, and currently learning Spanish.

How I Think About Sustainability

A working playbook, distilled from the Substack writing; nine beliefs, how the evidence gets weighed, and what he's careful not to claim.

  1. The risk outlives the brand. "ESG is dead" describes a label and some mandates, not exposure; weather losses, supply-chain emissions and claim liability didn't retreat with the rules.
  2. Practice first, paper second. A report is the receipt for work that happened, not a substitute for it. Reporting without doing exposes a company to buyers, lenders and regulators; doing without reporting exposes it to the first customer who asks. See "Pipeline Before Paper" and "The Wars of the Roses".
  3. Receipts beat reports. Every number should carry its source, its quality tier, and the action it triggered. Data is the binding constraint, and the biggest number, Scope 3, is usually the weakest. See "Receipts, Not Reports" and "In a Fit of Absence of Mind"; built directly, not just argued, in ESG Meets CPG, where every regulatory data row carries a source and a confidence label instead of a single invented number.
  4. Agents take the mechanical half; people keep the judgment half. Unit conversion, factor mapping, cross-framework translation and first drafts suit agents. Materiality, assurance and the signature stay human. See "Can Agentic AI Close CPG's ESG Reporting Gap?" and "Beyond the Reporting Chapter".
  5. Delegate only with a verification loop. The fault line isn't AI versus humans, it's delegation with verification versus without. Every agent output is a draft until a named person signs it. See "The Judgment Trade" and "The Singularity Tax".
  6. Value compounds only through shared infrastructure. One-off automations pay once; a shared data and agent layer lowers the cost of the next four. See "The Compounding Case".
  7. Buy results someone independent can check. The move from seats to outcomes suits sustainability, but only once a company has defined its own numbers; a faster report isn't a better one. See "The Seat Is Dead, Long Live the Invoice".
  8. Build standing systems, not annual campaigns. Always-on monitoring, a rotation between agents and people, and a named owner for every alert. See "Burhs, Not Heroes" and "Aut Caesar, Aut Nihil".
  9. Audit the AI as well. It has its own footprint, and it earns trust through auditability and narrow, revocable authority, not blind faith. See "The Singularity Tax" and "The Trust Threshold".

How he reasons: grades evidence by source (vendor, Big Four and survey figures are labelled as such, forecasts as forecasts); uses history as a lens on the shape of a problem, not as proof of the outcome; and spends attention on a number with a source before commentary that needs none. See "The Attention Standard".

What he wouldn't claim: that agents cut emissions by themselves; that AI makes materiality, assurance or sign-off disappear; that the agentic evidence is settled, most of it still comes from vendors and secondary surveys; or that "Goal-as-a-Service" is an established term rather than his own shorthand.
